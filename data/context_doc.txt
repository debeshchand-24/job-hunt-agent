CV Point: Content-Led Acquisition Engine | ₹4 Cr Quarterly Revenue

THE CV POINT
Built a content-led acquisition engine generating ~₹4 Cr quarterly revenue at significantly lower CAC than performance marketing channels

THE MASTER STORY (90-Second Verbal Version)
"At upGrad, I was leading growth and acquisition and we'd hit a real ceiling on performance marketing — CAC was climbing, returns were diminishing, and the honest view was that we couldn't spend our way to sustainable growth anymore.
When I mapped the user journey, I found a structural gap: upGrad had zero presence in the discovery phase of the education journey. Users only found us when they'd already decided to buy. We were invisible to anyone still figuring out what they wanted to learn.
We had a blog platform that had been largely neglected — it existed but had poor conversion architecture, infrequent publishing, and outdated content. It was generating traffic but converting almost none of it. Alongside that, there was no free learning resource at all — no tutorials, no short courses, nothing for users at the top of the funnel who weren't ready to buy.
I led a cross-functional team of 8 to build this out on two tracks simultaneously. For free courses and tutorials — a complete ground-up build: platform design, content production system, smooth discovery journeys, registration flows, and conversion nudges built into the learning experience from day one. For blogs — a revival: new conversion architecture, contextual lead capture, refreshed content, and significantly increased publishing frequency through an agency model with in-house audit.
The ramp was slow — 6 to 9 months to reach 5 lakh monthly visitors across both platforms, which is the nature of SEO. But the conversion profiles were distinct. Free courses converted at 22% visitor-to-lead at launch — high because users had already committed to starting a course. Blogs converted at 0.8% — lower but at much higher volume. Over the next 6 months, a dedicated conversion workstream moved those numbers to 30% and 1.5% respectively.
By month 12, the engine hit steady state: roughly 700 leads per day across both platforms — 450 from free courses and 225 from blogs — converting at 0.6% and 0.3% respectively, at an AOV of ₹2 lakh — approximately ₹6 Cr in incremental quarterly revenue, entirely from users we couldn't reach through paid channels. It has delivered consistently since.
What I'd do differently: I'd have invested in diversifying content formats earlier — video, structured learning paths — rather than going deeper on text-based content alone. And I'd have built the attribution infrastructure that made this channel's value legible to leadership from month one rather than month six."

Third, the compounding dynamic. Paid channels have a linear cost curve — spend X, get Y leads, every time. Content has a front-loaded cost with returns that accumulate over time. For a company already feeling CAC pressure, building something that gets cheaper per lead as it matures was strategically the right call."

"When I took ownership of upGrad's digital acquisition funnel, blended visitor-to-lead conversion was sitting at 3% — 2% on paid, 8% on organic. The platform was built around the business's perspective, not the user's. It presented as a course catalogue that overwhelmed visitors, had a multi-step lead capture flow that created unnecessary friction, and made no distinction between what a paid visitor needed versus what an organic visitor needed. The result was high CPLs, unsustainable CAC, and a growth model under real pressure.
I started this workstream alone — no dedicated team, just me coordinating across marketing, sales, programs, engineering, and design. This wasn't my only responsibility — across my three years at upGrad I was running multiple growth workstreams in parallel — but conversion optimisation was a consistent thread I returned to and built up over time. The total focused effort across the programme was roughly 3 to 4 quarters.
The work happened in three phases, each experiment-validated. First, a CRO workstream — we did deep user research: spoke to converted learners, interviewed leads who didn't convert, listened to counsellor calls, watched session recordings, analysed heatmaps. We rearchitected the lead capture flow into a multi-step journey with one action per step, ran systematic experiments on CTA placements, copies, and intent-specific combinations, and closed drop-off points analytically. This ran for about 6 months and had the single biggest impact on conversion.
Second, a full website redesign — validated through category-level A/B redirect experiments. We rebuilt the page architecture around user decision-making parameters rather than the business's internal product structure, introduced a three-step discovery journey — Home, Category, Program — and redesigned each page's story around the job to be done at that stage.
Third, a channel-specific landing strategy — we completely separated the experience for paid and organic traffic. Paid pages became short, salesy, conversion-focused with minimal scroll and no distractions. Organic pages became content-rich, answering every decision-making question a user might have. This drove a significant jump in paid conversion without touching organic performance.
The result: blended V2L moved from 3% to 12%. Paid went from 2% to 9%, organic from 8% to 20%. On comparable visitor volumes, this unlocked approximately ₹20 Cr in incremental quarterly revenue.
The hardest part wasn't the product work — it was managing CXO-level impatience with experimentation timelines. My solution was to convert the programme into a milestone-based plan with explicit targets at each checkpoint, so leadership had something meaningful to evaluate at defined intervals rather than expecting weekly movement in a metric that needed months to shift.
What I'd do differently: establish the experimentation framework and statistical rigour standards at the very start, and align leadership on those standards before the first experiment ran. Half our internal debates were about whether results were significant — debates that were entirely avoidable."

Q4: "Why free trial specifically? What other conversion mechanisms did you consider?"
Tests: Product Sense, Judgment
"We considered three alternatives before landing on free trial.
Money-back guarantee was the first option — it removes financial risk without requiring product investment upfront. We rejected it because in an edtech context at low ticket sizes, the operational overhead of processing refunds and the psychological complexity of 'I need to ask for my money back' creates more friction than it removes. Detailed preview content — extended trailers, sample modules as marketing content — was the second option. The problem is that produced preview content is curated and promotional by nature. Users had seen enough polished course trailers to be skeptical. What they wanted was unfiltered access to the actual learning experience.
Freemium — permanently free access to a limited content tier — was the third option. The problem with freemium at our stage was that it required a content strategy built around the free tier, and we hadn't designed our short courses with that architecture. It also changes the product positioning in ways that are hard to reverse.
Free trial won because it directly addressed the stated user concern — 'I want to sample before I commit' — without permanently restructuring the business model. The 7-day time gate and 50% content unlock created natural urgency and a clear conversion moment. Q5: "Walk me through the free trial conversion mechanics — how did you actually convert trial users to paying customers?"
Tests: Product Depth, Execution Detail
"The conversion architecture had four layers working together.
First, the content gate and time gate created natural urgency without manufactured pressure. Users had 7 days and access to 50% of the content including the hero modules — the most compelling material. The design intent was that users who engaged seriously would hit the content boundary at a moment of genuine motivation to continue. The time gate ensured that motivation didn't decay passively.
Second, we built a communication sequence around engagement milestones within the trial — not generic day-3 and day-6 reminder emails, but triggers based on actual user behaviour. If a user completed a module, the next communication acknowledged that progress and surfaced what was waiting beyond the trial. If a user hadn't engaged by day 3, the communication was different — a re-engagement hook focused on the specific outcome the course was promising.
Third, mid-funnel hooks on the platform itself — contextual conversion nudges at moments of high engagement, not just on a persistent banner. When a user finished a module and was in a positive state, that was the moment to surface the payment prompt.
Fourth, for short courses priced above ₹10K — a segment where the consideration level was higher — we maintained a lightweight sales touchpoint for high-intent unconverted users. Not the full counsellor model, but a tailored outreach for users who'd engaged meaningfully with the trial but hadn't converted.
The blended result: 20% free trial to payment conversion, L2P moving from 15% to 16.5%, and overall V2P from 4.5% to 5.9%."

Q6: "You mentioned this opened doors to free trial for high-ticket courses. What's the hypothesis there and what are the challenges?"

"The hypothesis is that the primary barrier to conversion on high-ticket programmes isn't price sensitivity — it's commitment anxiety. A ₹2 lakh programme is a significant life decision. Second, the trial design is harder. 7 days and 50% content works for a 20-hour short course. For a 12-month MBA programme, what does a meaningful trial look like? Too short and it doesn't give users enough to evaluate. Too long and it creates a free-rider problem.
Third, the sales motion changes. High-ticket programmes have a counsellor-assisted conversion model that needs to integrate with the trial experience — the trial isn't replacing the counsellor, it's informing the counsellor conversation.
I see this as a 12-18 month initiative, not a quick extension. The short course model is the proof of concept; the high-ticket version requires a fundamentally different design."

WATCH-OUTS
The owner funding model is your most defensible competitive moat claim — explain it precisely. Most loyalty programmes are funded by the platform. Wizard's owner-funded model means OYO's margin was protected while members got genuine value. That's a structurally superior design and interviewers at Swiggy and Uber — both of whom run platform-funded loyalty programmes — will find this genuinely interesting.
Know the LTV decomposition cold. 2.2x is the headline. The decomposition — 60% from frequency lift, remainder from tenure extension — is what makes it credible. Practice walking through the calculation from first principles: ₹264 per booking, 4 to 6.4 bookings per year, 18 to 24 month tenure.
The ₹99 pricing rationale will be probed. Don't say "it felt right" or "we tested a few prices." The answer is the commitment psychology argument — low enough for impulse purchase at checkout, high enough to create genuine behavioural pull to extract value.
Don't conflate membership revenue with LTV impact. ₹10 Cr membership revenue sounds modest. ₹1,900 Cr in incremental LTV created across 1M members is the transformative number. Lead with LTV in strategic conversations, use membership revenue as the supporting metric.
The Base Property mechanic will invite gaming questions. Have the fraud prevention answer ready — one Base Property per membership period, discount only at the designated property, and the economic model makes gaming unattractive because the 10% discount at Base still generates positive economics for the owner through repeat volume

Q1. "Tell me about your biggest failure and what you learned."
Story: Low-Commitment CTA Experiment
"At upGrad, I was running the digital conversion optimisation programme — trying to move blended visitor-to-lead conversion which was sitting at 3%. One of the experiments I was most confident about was low-commitment CTAs — replacing direct enrolment prompts with softer entry points like downloadable resources and single-field phone number captures. The hypothesis was clean: reduce friction at the top, increase lead volume, and the revenue math would work out.
What happened was the opposite of what I expected. V2L improved — we were capturing more leads. But L2P dropped meaningfully. The sales team was drowning in low-intent leads — people who'd downloaded a data science handbook out of mild curiosity, not people considering a ₹2 lakh programme. Sales bandwidth was being consumed by leads that were never going to convert, at the expense of the high-intent leads who were. Net revenue didn't improve — in some periods it declined.
The failure was mine specifically. I'd optimised for one metric without adequately modelling the downstream effect on another. I treated V2L and L2P as independent variables when they're deeply connected in a high-consideration category.
I killed the experiment, rebuilt our CTA strategy around intent signals rather than volume, and introduced quality filters into lead capture — CTAs calibrated to where a user was in their decision journey, not just to maximising form submissions.
What I learned: in a high-ticket, high-consideration product, lead quality and lead volume are not separate optimisation targets. The funnel is a system. Optimising one stage without understanding its effect on the next is how you create problems that take months to diagnose — and that's a lesson I've applied to every growth experiment I've run since."
Q2. "Tell me about a time you influenced without authority."
Story: Content Engine CEO Buy-In
"At upGrad, I needed to convince the CEO of the B2C business to invest in a content-led acquisition channel — blogs, free courses, tutorials — that had a 12-month payback horizon. This was during a period of significant CAC pressure when every rupee of investment was being scrutinised, and the instinct in the organisation was to double down on paid channels that showed results within the week.
The complication was structural. I had no authority over the budget decision — that sat with the CEO. And content is the hardest channel to sell internally because the value is invisible for months. Traffic doesn't monetise immediately. Rankings take time. Every week without visible results is a conversation about whether the investment is working.
My insight was that the wrong argument to make was an opportunity argument. Telling a CEO under CAC pressure that content is a great long-term opportunity invites the response 'we don't have time for long-term.' The right argument was a risk argument — paid is already showing structural diminishing returns, and if we don't build an alternative channel now, our CAC problem gets significantly worse in 12 months. I reframed the conversation from 'why content' to 'what's our alternative.'
I also structured the ask to minimise perceived risk. Contained team of 8, leveraged existing course content we'd already produced, and set the first milestone as traffic and lead quality at 6 months — not revenue. I asked the CEO to evaluate us against milestones, not against weekly conversion metrics.
The channel hit steady state at month 12 — ₹6 Cr quarterly revenue at a fraction of paid channel CAC, and it's been consistent since.
What I learned: influencing upward isn't about making the best case for your idea. It's about understanding what the other person is afraid of and showing them your solution addresses that fear.

Q3. "Tell me about a conflict with a senior stakeholder and how you resolved it."
Story: Paid vs Organic Page Separation
"When I proposed separating our paid and organic landing page experiences entirely — different pages for the same product depending on traffic source — I walked into three simultaneous objections from three different functions, each with a legitimate concern.
The SEO team's concern was technical and serious: duplicate content on pages for the same product would trigger Google penalties and hurt our organic rankings — the very channel we were trying to protect. The content ops head's concern was operational: maintaining two pages per product meant duplicated creation and management effort across hundreds of products. The marketing head's concern was oversight: monitoring and keeping two page variants current was more work than they'd signed up for.
Each objection was legitimate. None of them were wrong. And I couldn't dismiss any of them because I needed all three functions to execute this successfully.
I addressed each separately with a specific solution rather than trying to win a single argument. For the SEO team: we made all paid landing pages no-follow, no-index — invisible to Google's crawler, so there was no duplicate content issue from a search perspective. That was a technical resolution that fully addressed their concern without compromising the strategy. For content ops: we built a cloning functionality in our CMS where any page could be cloned with all content and widgets inherited from the source page — the incremental effort to create a paid variant of an existing organic page dropped to minutes, not hours. For the marketing head: I did the math on monitoring overhead versus conversion upside. The incremental effort to keep two pages current was genuinely small — a few additional checks per week per product. Against a paid conversion improvement of 4-5 percentage points, that was an obvious trade-off once it was quantified.
Paid V2L went from roughly 4-5% to 9% after implementation. None of the three concerns materialised.
What I learned: when you face simultaneous multi-stakeholder resistance, the instinct is to find one argument that addresses everyone. That rarely works because each person's concern is genuinely different. The more effective approach is to treat each objection as a separate problem to be solved — and solve them specifically."
Q4. "Tell me about a time you made a significant decision with incomplete data."
Story: Offline Channel Expansion
"When upGrad decided to move into offline — physical stores and admission centres — we had exactly 3 stores of pilot data and a hypothesis that a meaningful segment of our potential learners were offline-first. That was it. No market sizing study, no competitor benchmarking for edtech specifically, no unit economics model for physical retail.
The complication was the scale of the decision. We weren't talking about a small experiment — the ask was to build the product infrastructure, the lead management system, the counsellor tooling, and the discovery experience to support a national rollout. That's a significant engineering and operational investment based on a 3-store signal.
The insight that gave me enough confidence to proceed was the nature of the signal we did have. The 3-store pilot wasn't showing marginal results — it was showing genuine footfall and interest in locations near tech parks and metro stations, with a lead quality that was visibly higher than our digital channels. And the qualitative signal from counsellors was consistent: people who walked in were closer to buying than people who filled a form online. That directional strength mattered more to me than the sample size.
I made the decision to proceed but structured the investment to match the uncertainty. I didn't ask for 40-store infrastructure upfront. I proposed building the platform to support 10 stores with the architecture to scale, and committed to a 6-month checkpoint with clear metrics — lead volume, L2P, revenue per store — before the next expansion decision.
In 10 months we scaled to 40+ stores, ₹10.8 Cr quarterly revenue, and offline L2P of 2% — 25% better than our digital channels.
What I learned: waiting for complete data before making significant decisions is itself a decision — usually the wrong one. The skill isn't eliminating uncertainty, it's structuring your bets so that the cost of being wrong is recoverable while the upside of being right is meaningful."

Q5. "Tell me about a time you killed or significantly changed direction on a project."
Story: Self-Checkout V1 → Free Trial Pivot
"When upGrad launched short courses — priced between ₹500 and ₹25,000 — I built the first self-checkout experience the company had ever had. V1 was a clean product-to-payment flow: browse the course, see the offering, pay. We launched it, and it worked — but conversion wasn't where it needed to be.
The instinct in that situation is to optimise the existing flow — improve the page, tweak the CTA, reduce steps. I deliberately didn't do that immediately. Instead I went back to users who'd visited and not converted and asked them directly what stopped them.
The answer was consistent and something I hadn't anticipated: in a market flooded with GenAI and tech courses, people couldn't distinguish between options and weren't willing to commit financially without sampling first — even at ₹500. We'd assumed that a low price point eliminated the evaluation need. Users told us clearly it didn't.
That insight required a more significant response than CTA optimisation. It required changing the fundamental conversion model — from direct purchase to free trial as the primary hook. Seven days of access, 50% content unlock including hero modules, with time and content gates creating a natural conversion moment. We rebuilt the entire entry experience around the trial rather than around the purchase.
This wasn't a minor iteration. It required rebuilding the onboarding flow, building trial state management, designing a new nurture sequence around engagement milestones, and coordinating with the content team on which modules to unlock. It was effectively a new product.
Free trial to payment conversion came in at 20%. V2P moved from 4.5% to 5.9%. Revenue lifted 32% on identical traffic.
What I learned: the hardest product decision isn't killing something that's failing — it's changing direction on something that's working adequately. V1 was converting. It just wasn't converting well enough. Recognising the difference between 'this needs optimisation' and 'this needs a different model' is a judgment call that requires genuine user evidence — not just instinct."

Q6. "Tell me about a time you built and motivated a team through a difficult period."
Story: Conversion Programme Under CXO Pressure
"The digital conversion optimisation programme at upGrad was a 3-year initiative that I ran alongside other growth workstreams — roughly 3 to 4 quarters of focused effort spread across that period. The work itself was hard. But the harder challenge was protecting the team's ability to do the work properly while CXO-level stakeholders were reviewing progress weekly and expecting visible movement in a metric that needed months to shift.
The pressure was real and sustained. Every week without a meaningful conversion movement invited questions — is this working, should we change approach, should we redirect the team. That kind of environment is corrosive for experimentation quality because it creates incentives to call results early, to chase short-term signals, and to context-switch before current work has had time to prove itself.
My job as the leader of this workstream was to absorb that pressure and not let it reach the team in a way that compromised their work. I did two things specifically.
First, I converted the programme into a milestone-based plan with explicit conversion targets at each checkpoint — and I presented this to leadership as the framework for evaluation. Instead of weekly conversion metrics, I gave them meaningful milestones: CRO workstream completion with target conversion range, website redesign with target range, channel separation with target range. That shifted the leadership conversation from 'why hasn't the number moved this week' to 'are you on track for the milestone.' Productive rather than reactive.
Second, I was transparent with the team about the pressure without transferring the anxiety. I told them directly: leadership is impatient, that's the environment, here's how I'm managing it, and here's what I need from you — rigour and patience with the process. Teams respond to honesty about context. What they can't function under is uncertainty about whether their work is being protected.
The programme delivered — blended conversion from 3% to 12%, ₹20 Cr incremental quarterly revenue. The team stayed intact and focused throughout.
What I learned: protecting your team from organisational pressure isn't about shielding them from reality. It's about absorbing the noise yourself, giving them a clear and stable framework to work within, and being honest about the environment without making it their problem to solve."

Q7. "Tell me about a time you had to prioritise ruthlessly when everything felt urgent."
Story: Conversion Workstream Phase Sequencing
"The digital conversion programme had three major phases — CRO workstream, full website redesign, and channel-specific landing strategy — and the instinct from leadership and the broader team was to run all three simultaneously. We had a metric that needed to move, we had three proven levers, and the pressure was to pull all of them at once.
The complication was that running all three simultaneously wasn't actually faster — it was slower and riskier. Each phase generated the evidence that made the next phase more effective. The CRO workstream told us what users actually needed at each funnel stage — without that evidence, the website redesign would have been built on assumptions rather than data. The redesign gave us the page architecture that made channel separation viable — without clean page foundations, splitting paid and organic experiences would have created inconsistency rather than improvement.
The sequencing dependency was real but invisible to stakeholders who just saw three workstreams that could theoretically run in parallel.
My decision was to sequence them deliberately and defend that sequencing explicitly. CRO workstream first — 6 months, user research driven, experiment validated. Website redesign second — built on CRO learnings, category-level A/B tested. Channel separation third — built on redesigned page foundations.
I presented this sequencing to leadership not as a capacity constraint but as a strategic choice — here is why phase 2 is more effective after phase 1, here is what we'd be giving up by running them simultaneously, here is the expected conversion impact of each phase in sequence versus in parallel.
The result: blended conversion from 3% to 12%, paid from 2% to 9%, organic from 8% to 20%. Every phase built on the previous one's evidence exactly as planned.
What I learned: ruthless prioritisation at senior level isn't just about what to work on — it's about sequencing. The right things done in the wrong order produce worse outcomes than a smaller set of things done in the right order. And the harder skill is convincing stakeholders that sequencing is a strategic choice, not a capacity excuse."

Q8. "Tell me about a time you used data to change someone's mind."
Story: Low-Commitment CTA Kill Decision
"During the digital conversion programme at upGrad, we ran a low-commitment CTA experiment — replacing direct enrolment prompts with softer entry points like downloadable resources and single-field phone number captures. The hypothesis was that reducing friction at the top of the funnel would increase lead volume enough to more than compensate for any quality drop.
Two weeks into the experiment, the V2L numbers looked good. Lead volume was up meaningfully. By the surface metric the experiment was working — and there was pressure from the growth team to call it a success and roll it out broadly.
My concern was the downstream data. L2P had started moving in the wrong direction. Not dramatically — but the direction was clear and the trend was early enough that most people were willing to attribute it to noise. I wasn't.
I built a simple model: if L2P continues declining at the current rate, what does net revenue look like at full rollout versus the control group? The answer was unambiguous — even at optimistic V2L assumptions, the L2P decline more than offset the lead volume gain. We were generating more activity and less revenue.
I presented this to the team and leadership with the full funnel model — not just V2L in isolation but V2L, L2P, and net revenue in a single view. The visual made the problem impossible to ignore. A metric that looked green at the top of the funnel was red at the bottom.
We killed the experiment. The pushback was real — "the lead numbers are good, give it more time" — but the model held. I held to the data.
What I learned: the most important use of data in product isn't proving that something works. It's catching early when something that looks like it's working is actually creating a downstream problem. That requires being willing to look at the full system, not just the metric that's moving in the right direction."
